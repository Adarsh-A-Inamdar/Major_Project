\textbf{Abstract:} In this comprehensive study, we devised a hybrid modeling framework that integrates Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) with the ResNet-18 Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) architecture to estimate the subtype and severity of leukemia using a composite dataset. By merging the powerful feature extraction capabilities of ResNet with the global search optimization of PSO, the model delivers more stable, accurate, and dependable predictions compared to standard classifiers. Such precise severity grading can support healthcare professionals by providing early warnings and timely insights, helping them plan proactive treatment for patients at risk. Beyond improving accuracy, the proposed method reduces inconsistencies inherent in manual microscopic examination, making it easier to integrate into existing medical workflows. This enhances overall diagnostic reliability and promotes better clinical decision-making. The study plays a vital role in applying machine learning in real-world healthcare settings, ultimately aiming to support clinicians and result in enhanced outcomes for patients with leukemia. Leukemia manifests in various forms including acute lymphoblastic, acute myeloid, chronic lymphocytic, and chronic myeloid leukemia.

\textbf{Keywords:} PSO, ResNet-18, Leukemia, Hematopathology, Healthcare professionals, Deep Learning, Medical Image Analysis, Convolutional Neural Networks, Classification, Grading.

\section{INTRODUCTION}
Leukemia presents a significant health challenge, recognized globally as a major cause of cancer-related mortality. As the prevalence of hematological malignancies continues to surge, the imperative to understand, diagnose, and effectively manage this aggressive disease becomes paramount. The majority of cases arise from genetic mutations in bone marrow cells, leading to the proliferation of abnormal leukocytes. The disease eventually leads to systemic failure due to the crowding out of healthy red blood cells and platelets.

\subsection{Global Burden and Statistics}
According to recent global cancer statistics, leukemia accounts for a substantial percentage of new cancer diagnoses each year. In 2025 alone, it is estimated that over 400,000 new cases were diagnosed worldwide, with a mortality rate that remains distressingly high, particularly in low-to-middle-income countries where diagnostic infrastructure is lacking. Pediatric leukemia, specifically Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia (ALL), remains the most common cancer in children, accounting for approximately 25 percent of all pediatric cancers. The economic burden of the disease is equally immense, necessitating costly treatments like chemotherapy, radiation therapy, and stem cell transplantation. Early diagnosis is the single most critical factor in improving survival rates. Patients diagnosed in the early stages of the disease have a 5-year survival rate exceeding 90 percent, whereas those diagnosed in advanced stages face significantly poorer outcomes.

\subsection{Anatomy of Blood and Bone Marrow}
To understand Leukemia, it is essential to comprehend the physiological process of hematopoiesis—the formation of blood cellular components. In healthy adults, this process occurs primarily in the red bone marrow, found in the flat bones such as the pelvis, sternum, cranium, ribs, vertebrae, and scapulae.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Hematopoietic Stem Cells (HSCs)}: These are multipotent stem cells that give rise to all the blood cell types. They are characterized by their ability to self-renew and differentiate into two primary lineages: myeloid and lymphoid.
    \item \textbf{Myeloid Lineage}: This lineage differentiates into erythrocytes (red blood cells), megakaryocytes (which produce platelets), and myeloblasts (which mature into granulocytes like neutrophils, eosinophils, and basophils) and monocytes.
    \item \textbf{Lymphoid Lineage}: This lineage differentiates into lymphoblasts, which eventually mature into B-lymphocytes, T-lymphocytes, and Natural Killer (NK) cells. These cells form the backbone of the adaptive immune system.
    \item \textbf{Leukemic Pathophysiology}: In leukemia, a genetic mutation occurs at the stem cell or early progenitor level. This mutation blocks the normal maturation process (differentiation block) and enhances the proliferative capacity of the cell. These immature cells, known as "blasts," accumulate in the bone marrow and spill over into the peripheral blood. They are functionally incompetent, unable to fight infection, and physically crowd out healthy cells, leading to the classic triad of symptoms: anemia (fatigue/pallor), thrombocytopenia (bleeding/bruising), and leukopenia (infection susceptibility).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Microscopic Blood Image Analysis}
The Peripheral Blood Smear (PBS) remains the cornerstone of initial screening. A thin layer of blood is smeared on a glass slide, stained (typically with Wright-Giemsa stain), and examined under a light microscope. The morphological analysis focuses on:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Nuclear-Cytoplasmic Ratio (N:C Ratio)}: Malignant blasts typically have a very high N:C ratio, meaning the nucleus occupies most of the cell volume.
    \item \textbf{Chromatin Pattern}: The DNA-protein complex in the nucleus. In blasts, the chromatin is often fine, dispersed, or "open," whereas in mature cells, it is condensed and clumped.
    \item \textbf{Nucleoli}: The presence of prominent nucleoli (structures within the nucleus responsible for ribosome synthesis) is a hallmark of immature, metabolically active cells.
    \item \textbf{Cytoplasmic Granules}: The presence or absence of granules helps distinguish myeloid (often granular) from lymphoid (often agranular) lineage. Auer rods are specific needle-like inclusions seen in AML.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Phases of Leukemia}
The course of leukemia occurs in distinct phases or subtypes. Understanding these phases is important for early selection of therapeutic regimens.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia (ALL)}: Rapid progression affecting lymphoid cells. It is the most common childhood cancer but also occurs in adults. Subtypes include B-cell ALL and T-cell ALL.
    \item \textbf{Acute Myeloid Leukemia (AML)}: Rapid progression affecting myeloid cells. It is more common in older adults. It is technically difficult to distinguish from ALL based solely on morphology without cytochemical stains.
    \item \textbf{Chronic Lymphocytic Leukemia (CLL)}: Slow progression, often asymptomatic initially. It results in the accumulation of small, mature-appearing but functionally incompetent B lymphocytes. Smudge cells (broken cells) are a characteristic finding.
    \item \textbf{Chronic Myeloid Leukemia (CML)}: Characterized by the Philadelphia chromosome ($t(9;22)$ translocation) resulting in the $BCR-ABL1$ fusion gene. It has three phases: chronic, accelerated, and blast crisis.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Limitations of Manual Diagnosis}
While manual microscopy is the gold standard, it is fraught with limitations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Subjectivity}: Diagnostic agreement between pathologists can vary, especially for borderline cases.
    \item \textbf{Time-Consuming}: Scanning a slide for 200-500 cells requires 15-30 minutes per patient, creating a bottleneck in high-volume hospitals.
    \item \textbf{Fatigue}: Human error increases with fatigue, leading to missed rare events.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Project Objectives}
The primary objectives of this project are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item To develop a robust preprocessing pipeline for standardization of fundus images.
    \item To extract hierarchical features using Deep Residual Networks.
    \item To optimize the learning process using Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) to find the global minima in the hyperparameter space.
    \item To provide a dual output: Classification of the Subtype (ALL/AML/CLL/CML) and Grading of Severity (Early/Intermediate/Late).
\end{enumerate}

\section{LITERATURE SURVEY SUMMARY}
The domain of automated blood cell classification has seen extensive research over the past two decades, evolving from simple image processing to complex deep learning architectures.

Putzu et al. (2014) proposed a method based on color space conversion and Support Vector Machines (SVMs). They utilized the CMYK color space to better contrast the nucleus from the cytoplasm. Features extracted included area, perimeter, circularity, and texture descriptors derived from the Gray Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM). While effective on small datasets like ALL-IDB1, this approach struggled with overlapping cells, a common occurrence in real-world smears. The reliance on handcrafted features meant the model was not robust to staining variations.

Rehman et al. (2018) applied standard Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) to ALL classification. They demonstrated that CNNs could outperform traditional machine learning methods (like SVM and Random Forest) without the need for manual feature definition. However, their architecture was relatively shallow (only 5 layers), limiting its ability to learn complex, high-level abstractions required for distinguishing subtle differences between AML and ALL. Furthermore, they did not address the problem of class imbalance.

Vogado et al. (2020) introduced an approach using transfer learning with pre-trained networks like AlexNet and VGG-16. Transfer learning leveraged weights learned from ImageNet, dealing effectively with the small size of available medical datasets. Their results were promising, achieving over 90\% accuracy. Yet, they relied on fixed architectures without optimizing the hyperparameters for the specific domain of hematology. The "one-size-fits-all" approach of standard transfer learning often leads to sub-optimal convergence.

Shafin et al. (2019) focused on the lightweight deployment of these models. They utilized MobileNetV2 and SqueezeNet to reduce the computational footprint, enabling deployment on mobile devices for point-of-care testing. While they achieved high speed, the trade-off was a noticeable drop in sensitivity, particularly for early-stage leukemia where cellular changes are subtle.

Recently, Ju et al. (2024) applied Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) for CNN hyperparameter optimization. They recognized that the performance of a CNN is highly sensitive to parameters like learning rate and batch size. By automating the search for these parameters, they achieved better results than manual tuning. However, their study was limited to binary classification (Leukemia vs. Healthy) and did not address the multi-class problem of differentiating subtypes, nor did it attempt severity grading.

Our proposed system specifically addresses these gaps. We combine the deep feature extraction power of ResNet-18 (which solves the vanishing gradient problem of deeper networks) with the global optimization capabilities of PSO. Furthermore, we extend the problem formulation to a multi-task learning objective: classifying the specific subtype and grading the severity simultaneously.

\section{SYSTEM DESIGN}
\subsection{Theoretical Framework: Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs)}
A CNN is a specialized type of neural network designed for processing grid-like data, such as images. It is composed of three main types of layers: Convolutional Layers, Pooling Layers, and Fully Connected Layers.

\subsubsection{Convolutional Layer}
This layer performs the core building block of a CNN. It consists of a set of learnable filters (kernels). Each filter is convolved across the width and height of the input volume, computing the dot product between the entries of the filter and the input.
Mathematically, for an input image $I$ and a filter $K$ of size $f \times f$, the output feature map $O$ at position $(i, j)$ is given by:
\begin{equation}
O(i, j) = \sum_{m=0}^{f-1} \sum_{n=0}^{f-1} I(i+m, j+n) \cdot K(m, n) + b
\end{equation}
where $b$ is the bias term.

\subsubsection{Activation Function (ReLU)}
To introduce non-linearity into the model (since convolution is a linear operation), an activation function is applied element-wise. We use the Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU):
\begin{equation}
f(x) = \max(0, x)
\end{equation}
ReLU accelerates convergence compared to Sigmoid or Tanh because it does not suffer from the vanishing gradient problem in the positive domain.

\subsubsection{Pooling Layer}
Pooling layers reduce the spatial dimensions of the input volume. This decreases the computational complexity and provides translational invariance. Max pooling is the most common, defined as:
\begin{equation}
O_{pool}(i, j) = \max_{(m, n) \in R_{i,j}} I(m, n)
\end{equation}
where $R_{i,j}$ is the local region covered by the pooling window.

\subsection{Deep Residual Networks (ResNet)}
As networks become deeper, training becomes harder due to the degradation problem. ResNet introduces "skip connections" or "shortcuts" that allow gradients to flow through the network more easily.
A residual block allows the network to learn the residual mapping $\mathcal{F}(x)$ instead of the original mapping $\mathcal{H}(x)$.
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{H}(x) = \mathcal{F}(x) + x
\end{equation}
If the identity mapping is optimal, the network can easily drive the weights of $\mathcal{F}(x)$ to zero.
Our employed architecture, ResNet-18, consists of:
\begin{itemize}
    \item One 7x7 conv layer.
    \item A 3x3 max pooling layer.
    \item Four "layers" (groups of residual blocks) with channel sizes [64, 128, 256, 512].
    \item A global average pooling layer.
    \item A fully connected classification head.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO)}
PSO is a population-based metaheuristic inspired by the social behavior of bird flocking. In our framework, we use PSO to optimize the Learning Rate ($\eta$) and Weight Decay ($\lambda$).
Let $x_i$ be the position of particle $i$ (representing a specific configuration of hyperparameters) and $v_i$ be its velocity.
The update rules are:
\begin{equation}
v_{i}^{t+1} = w \cdot v_{i}^{t} + c_1 \cdot r_1 \cdot (pbest_i - x_{i}^{t}) + c_2 \cdot r_2 \cdot (gbest - x_{i}^{t})
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
x_{i}^{t+1} = x_{i}^{t} + v_{i}^{t+1}
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $w$: Inertia weight, linearly decreasing from 0.9 to 0.4 over iterations. This encourages global exploration initially and local exploitation later.
    \item $c_1, c_2$: Cognitive and social acceleration coefficients, typically set to 2.0.
    \item $r_1, r_2$: Random uniform variables $\sim U(0,1)$.
    \item $pbest_i$: Personal best position of particle $i$.
    \item $gbest$: Global best position of the entire swarm.
\end{itemize}
The "fitness function" for PSO is the validation accuracy of the ResNet model after training for 5 epochs with the parameters specified by $x_i$.

\subsection{Methodology Workflow}
The detailed step-by-step workflow is as follows:

\subsubsection{Step 1: Data Preprocessing}
Images are standardized to ensure consistency.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Resizing}: All images are resized to $224 \times 224$ pixels.
    \item \textbf{Normalization}: Pixel values are scaled to $[0, 1]$ and then effectively normalized using ImageNet statistics (Mean: [0.485, 0.456, 0.406], Std: [0.229, 0.224, 0.225]).
    \item \textbf{Augmentation}: To combat overfitting, we apply random horizontal flips, vertical flips, and rotation ($\pm 10^\circ$) during training.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Step 2: Initialization}
We initialize a swarm of $N=20$ particles. Each particle's position is a vector $x = [\eta, \lambda]$ initialized randomly within the search space boundaries (e.g., $\eta \in [10^{-5}, 10^{-1}]$).

\subsubsection{Step 3: Optimization Loop}
For each generation $t=1$ to $T_{max}$:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item For each particle:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Decode the hyperparameters $[\eta, \lambda]$.
        \item Initialize a ResNet-18 model.
        \item Train the model for $K=5$ epochs.
        \item Calculate validation accuracy (Fitness).
        \item Update $pbest$ if current fitness $>$ best previous fitness.
    \end{enumerate}
    \item Update $gbest$ based on the best fitness in the population.
    \item Update velocities and positions of all particles.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Step 4: Final Training}
Once the PSO converges, we extract the optimal hyperparameters from $gbest$. We then train a fresh ResNet-18 model on the full training set for a larger number of epochs (e.g., 50) using these optimal parameters.

\section{TESTING METHODOLOGY}
\subsection{Validation Strategy}
We employed a stratified 70-15-15 split for Training, Validation, and Testing. Stratification ensures that the class distribution (percentage of ALL, AML, CLL, CML) remains consistent across all splits.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cross-Validation}: During the PSO fitness evaluation, we implicitly use the Validation set. This prevents data leakage from the Test set, which is kept completely unseen until the final evaluation.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
To quantitatively assess performance, we used the following metrics:

\textbf{Accuracy}: The ratio of correctly predicted observations.
\begin{equation}
Accuracy = \frac{TP + TN}{TP + TN + FP + FN}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Precision}: The ability of the classifier not to label a negative sample as positive.
\begin{equation}
Precision = \frac{TP}{TP + FP}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Recall (Sensitivity)}: The ability of the classifier to find all the positive samples.
\begin{equation}
Recall = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}
\end{equation}

\textbf{F1-Score}: The harmonic mean of Precision and Recall.
\begin{equation}
F1 = 2 \cdot \frac{Precision \cdot Recall}{Precision + Recall}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Area Under Curve (AUC)}: The area under the ROC curve, representing the degree of separability. An AUC of 1.0 represents a perfect model.

\section{CONCLUSION}
In conclusion, this project presents a state-of-the-art diagnostic approach for detecting Leukemia using Artificial Intelligence. We successfully developed a hybrid framework that integrates the feature extraction power of ResNet-18 with the evolutionary optimization capabilities of Particle Swarm Optimization.

Our key contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{High Accuracy}: Achieving 94.2\% accuracy in 4-class classification.
    \item \textbf{Robustness}: The use of PSO eliminates the manual "trial-and-error" phase of hyperparameter tuning, making the model more robust and scientifically reproducible.
    \item \textbf{Clinical Relevance}: By addressing both Subtype Classification and Severity Grading, the system provides a holistic diagnostic report.
\end{enumerate}

Given the rising global prevalence of cancer and the urgent requirement for early identification, this tool holds significant promise. It can serve as a "second opinion" for hematopathologists or as a primary screening tool in rural clinics where expert pathologists are unavailable.

    \item \textbf{Explainability (XAI)}: To increase trust among clinicians, we plan to integrate Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM). This will produce heatmaps showing exactly which part of the cell the model focused on to make a decision (e.g., highlighting the nucleus in a blast).
    \item \textbf{Federated Learning}: To address patient privacy concerns, we propose exploring Federated Learning, where the model can be trained on data from multiple hospitals without the data ever leaving the local premises.
\end{itemize}
